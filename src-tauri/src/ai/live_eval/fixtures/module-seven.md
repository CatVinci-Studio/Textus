# Please don't answer

On the third winter night on the far side of the Moon, Lin Chi received a text from Earth: “Don't open Module Seven. I'm you, tomorrow.”

The timestamp was nineteen hours ahead. She checked the antenna, restarted the terminal, and gave the decoder, which was older than she was, a few taps. The message stayed. There was a voice recording attached, too. Even the faint whistle in the speaker's breath sounded exactly like hers.

“You'll hear someone knocking. Don't answer.”

She was alone at the base. After the others had left aboard the transport ship, she had stayed to look after the last batch of lunar soil. Module Seven held a glasslike mineral. Under a lamp, tiny points of light appeared inside it, as though someone had crushed a starry sky and packed it into the stone.

The knocking began at two in the morning.

Three soft taps. Then her mother's voice: “Chi, your dinner's getting cold.”

Her mother had been dead for eleven years. Lin Chi pressed her palms against the desk until her knuckles stopped trembling. The voice outside became the commander's, then her childhood classmate's, and finally her own. Each said only one sentence, then waited patiently.

The terminal lit up again: “It has no voice. It borrows the voices in your memory.”

“How do you know?” Lin Chi typed.

This time, the reply took a long while: “Because I answered.”

The observation window of Module Seven slowly brightened. The glasslike mineral was gone. Beyond the window was a kitchen. Her mother stood with her back to her, chopping vegetables. The knife struck the cutting board without a sound. Someone in orange work clothes sat at the table, typing on a terminal.

The figure turned. It was Lin Chi, much older.

At last she understood. The messages hadn't traveled nineteen hours through time. Whatever was in the module had folded certain moments together. The timestamp was only the decoder's best attempt to make sense of it.

“How long have you been in there?” she asked.

“Don't ask.” The words appeared on the screen, then vanished. “Shut off the base's power. It uses our answers to find its way, even our texts.”

Lin Chi looked toward the main switch. Cutting the power would also cut the heat. The rescue ship was still nineteen hours away.

The woman beyond the window took off her coat, revealing an old sweater just like hers. Her mother set dinner on the table and gently touched the woman's shoulder. Lin Chi could almost remember the warmth rising from the rice.

She put on her spacesuit, connected an independent oxygen cylinder, and walked to the main switch.

The screen lit up one last time: “Can you really bear to leave?”

Lin Chi said nothing and pulled the switch.

In the dark, three more knocks sounded at the door. She began counting her breaths.

Nineteen hours later, the rescue team found her beside the airlock, her oxygen almost gone. The mineral was still in Module Seven, but every point of light inside it had gone out.
