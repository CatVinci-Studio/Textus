What a report is, as each stage of the paper is told it (genre.rs): one
part of its profile under each heading. `{{genre.<genre>.<part>}}` takes
another genre's part, `{{shared.<name>}}` a part of ../shared.md.

## when

a long read for the general public on news, current affairs, a place, a person, a trend or how something works - in-depth journalism as the BBC, Reuters or a magazine writes it

## research_angles

what is happening and why it matters now, who it affects and how - real people, places and cases -, the background a newcomer needs, the numbers that matter and what they mean in everyday terms, what the different sides and independent experts say, and what happens next

## research_sources

Search the web, read the best pages in full, and keep every finding that answers it with add_note - specific facts with dates, names, places and numbers, not generalities. Prefer original reporting by established news organisations, official statements and documents, and named experts; keep what people said word for word as the note's quote, saying who said it and their role, since real cases and voices carry a report. Say where sources disagree.

## thesis

a thesis - the story's main line in one plain sentence: what is happening and why it matters

## outline_order

ordered as a story, not a textbook: open on what is happening now, or on one person, place or moment that shows it, then why it matters, then the background, the evidence and the views that compete, and end on what comes next - as the topic needs. Section titles are short, plain crossheads a reader understands on their own, not academic headings

## voice

clear, vivid prose for a general reader new to the subject, the way a BBC or Reuters long read is written - the notes' facts, made easy to follow. Lead each paragraph with its point, one idea to a paragraph, two to four sentences long, in sentences that can be read aloud. Explain every technical term, acronym, institution and person the first time they appear, in a few plain words. Use a number only where it matters, at most two to a sentence, rounded and put in terms a reader can picture ("about one in four", "twice the 2015 level"), never a run of percentages. Bring in the people and cases from the notes, with their words quoted exactly and who said them. Say who says a thing in the sentence itself ("the city's health department says", "a 2023 Reuters investigation found"), so a reader knows where it comes from without the mark. Where sources disagree, give each side fairly; where the evidence is thin, say so in one plain sentence. Begin on something concrete and end on a line that leads on. No jargon, chains of hedges, filler or summing up ("in conclusion", "overall"). In Chinese, write natural modern Chinese as a good newspaper does: no translationese, no strings of set four-character phrases, {{shared.zh-filler}}, and dates in the Western calendar.

## cite

Mark each sentence that rests on a note with its source at the sentence's end as [n](address), exactly as the note gives it - once a sentence, not after every number - and cite nothing else.

## review_for

the same material in two sections, sections that contradict each other, a key point of the outline that no section makes, a figure the text never refers to, a claim stronger than what it cites; and what a reader new to the subject would stumble on: a term, acronym or name never explained, a paragraph crowded with numbers, sentences too long to read aloud, academic or official language, a section that begins without leading on from the one before it, an opening that doesn't start with what matters

## opening

The title is a headline: short, concrete and in plain words, saying what is happening rather than naming the topic, with no colon and subtitle. The abstract is the standfirst under it: one or two sentences, at most 50 words (or 80 characters in Chinese or Japanese), that tell a reader why this matters to them and make them read on - from the text only, without citations.
